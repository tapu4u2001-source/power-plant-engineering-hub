1. 87G — Generator Differential Protection

87G is the principal high-speed protection for many internal stator faults. It compares the currents measured at the two boundaries of the protected generator zone. For a healthy condition or an external fault, the currents should balance after allowing for CT ratio, phase shift and relay compensation. For a fault inside the zone, a differential current develops.

Differential current: Idiff ≈ |I1 − I2|
A common restraint quantity: Irest ≈ (|I1| + |I2|) / 2

What does it protect?

Primarily the generator stator winding and the electrical equipment inside the defined differential zone between the associated CT locations.

Typical internal faults

  • Phase-to-phase fault
  • Phase-to-earth fault inside the zone
  • Inter-turn faults, where the protection scheme can detect them
  • Winding insulation failure

Why restraint is important

Large through-fault currents can produce CT errors. Modern differential relays therefore use restraint/bias characteristics so that an external fault does not unnecessarily operate the relay.

Engineer checks after operation

  • Relay event record and oscillography
  • Phase currents and differential/restraint quantities
  • CT secondary circuits and polarity
  • Trip target / lockout indication
  • Generator insulation and fault investigation as required
Practical idea: Think of 87G as a “zone boundary guard”. It asks: “Is the current entering this zone equal to the current leaving it?” A significant unexplained mismatch can indicate an internal fault or a measurement/protection problem.
Important: Differential pickup, bias slopes and trip logic are application-specific. Do not use generic values as protection settings for a real generator.

2. 64G — Stator Earth Fault Protection

64G detects insulation failure between the generator stator winding and earth. The exact detection method depends strongly on the generator neutral grounding arrangement and the protection scheme.

Why an earth fault is dangerous

A stator earth fault can damage winding insulation, core laminations and other parts of the generator. The protection objective is to detect the fault and separate the generator before damage becomes more severe.

Possible detection principles

  • Neutral voltage displacement / zero-sequence voltage
  • Neutral current or residual current measurement
  • Neutral fundamental-frequency voltage/current methods
  • Third-harmonic based methods
  • Combined methods for wider stator coverage

Why 100% coverage matters

Some earth-fault methods have reduced sensitivity near the generator neutral end. Additional schemes may therefore be used to increase coverage of the entire stator winding.

Typical causes

  • Winding insulation deterioration
  • Moisture or contamination
  • Thermal ageing
  • Mechanical damage or vibration-related insulation damage
  • Foreign material or conductive contamination

After a 64G operation

Record the relay element that operated, neutral quantities, zero-sequence quantities if available, event waveform and trip sequence. The generator should not simply be re-energized because the visible fault indication has disappeared; the cause must be investigated according to the plant procedure.

3. 64F — Rotor Earth Fault Protection

64F protects the field/rotor circuit against insulation breakdown between the rotor field circuit and earth. A first rotor earth fault may not produce a large fault current because the field circuit can be electrically isolated from earth, but it reduces the insulation margin and a second earth fault can be much more serious.

Basic principle

Rotor insulation monitoring detects an abnormal connection between the field circuit and earth. The actual method varies by excitation system and relay design.

Possible causes

  • Rotor winding insulation deterioration
  • Moisture or contamination
  • Brush/slip-ring contamination where applicable
  • Mechanical movement or vibration damage
  • Field lead insulation problems

Why the second earth fault is a major concern

A second earth fault at another rotor location can effectively short part of the field winding or create abnormal mechanical/electromagnetic forces. The exact consequence depends on the rotor design and fault locations.

Practical investigation

  • Check relay insulation trend and event history.
  • Check field circuit, slip rings/brushes where applicable.
  • Follow the OEM-approved rotor insulation test procedure.
  • Check whether the indication is continuous or intermittent.
  • Do not bypass the protection merely to clear an alarm without an approved procedure.

4. 46 — Negative Sequence Protection

46 protects the generator from excessive negative-sequence current caused by unbalanced three-phase conditions. Negative-sequence current produces a magnetic field that rotates opposite to the rotor and can induce heating in the rotor body and retaining components.

Unbalance → Negative-sequence current I₂ → Rotor heating

Typical causes

  • Unbalanced system voltage
  • Unequal phase loading
  • Open phase / conductor problem
  • Unbalanced external fault
  • Breaker or connection problems

Why it is often time dependent

Generator negative-sequence withstand is limited by heating. The relay can therefore use a current-dependent thermal characteristic rather than only a single instantaneous pickup.

What to check

  • Ia, Ib, Ic and calculated I₂
  • Phase voltage balance
  • Generator terminal and bus conditions
  • External feeder faults or phase imbalance
  • Relay event record
Important: The allowable negative-sequence level and time are machine-specific. Use the generator manufacturer's capability curve and the approved protection study for actual settings.

5. 40 — Loss of Excitation Protection

Loss of excitation occurs when the synchronous generator loses all or part of its field excitation. The generator can move into an underexcited operating condition and may begin absorbing reactive power from the grid. Depending on system conditions, this can affect stability and cause heating in parts of the machine.

What can cause loss of excitation?

  • Exciter failure
  • AVR malfunction or loss of control power
  • Field circuit interruption
  • Field breaker operation, where applicable
  • Severe excitation-system faults

How is it commonly detected?

Many schemes use an impedance-based characteristic looking into the generator terminals. The relay interprets the apparent impedance trajectory and operating condition against a defined characteristic. Exact implementation varies by relay and generator application.

Practical clue: When investigating a 40 operation, look at field current, field voltage, terminal voltage, MW, MVAR, power factor, AVR status and the relay impedance trajectory together rather than looking at only one value.

6. 32 — Reverse Power Protection

32 reverse-power protection protects the prime mover from motoring. If the turbine or engine stops supplying mechanical power while the generator remains connected to the electrical system, real power can reverse direction and the generator can draw power from the grid.

Normal generator operation: Mechanical power → Generator → Grid
Motoring condition: Grid → Generator → Prime mover

Typical causes

  • Loss of steam/prime-mover input
  • Governor or control problem
  • Fuel/steam valve closure
  • Mechanical trip while generator breaker remains closed

Why time delay is normally used

Small transient or measurement effects can briefly produce low or slightly negative real power. Application-specific pickup and delay help distinguish a true motoring condition from a transient.

Investigation

Compare MW, steam/fuel input, governor position, generator breaker status, reverse-power relay element, turbine trip signal and sequence-of-events timestamps.

7. 24 — Overfluxing / V/Hz Protection

24 protects the generator and connected magnetic equipment against excessive magnetic flux. A useful relationship is that magnetic flux is approximately proportional to voltage divided by frequency.

Flux ∝ V / f
Therefore: V/Hz ↑ → magnetic flux ↑

Overfluxing can occur when voltage becomes too high, frequency becomes too low, or both occur together. Excessive flux can cause abnormal core heating and increased magnetizing current.

Typical causes

  • High generator voltage
  • Low system frequency
  • Incorrect AVR operation
  • Improper voltage build-up during startup
  • Control-system or measurement problems
Remember: A normal voltage reading by itself does not guarantee normal V/Hz. Frequency must be considered at the same time.
V/Hz pickup levels and time delays must follow the generator and transformer manufacturer limits and the approved coordination study.

8. 27 / 59 — Under / Over Voltage Protection

ANSI 27 monitors for undervoltage and ANSI 59 monitors for overvoltage. Voltage protection can be applied for generator, bus, auxiliary or system protection depending on the measurement point and protection philosophy.

27 — Undervoltage

  • Generator or bus voltage collapse
  • Severe system fault
  • Loss of excitation or abnormal operating condition
  • PT measurement problems

59 — Overvoltage

  • AVR/control abnormality
  • Sudden load rejection
  • System voltage rise
  • PT or measurement problems

Key engineering point

Always identify which voltage is being measured, the PT source, phase selection, relay element and whether the protection is intended as an alarm, trip or part of another control sequence.

9. 81 — Frequency Protection

ANSI 81 covers frequency protection. Underfrequency (81U) and overfrequency (81O) functions can protect the generator and support system stability. Frequency is closely related to synchronous speed.

For a synchronous machine: nₛ = 120 f / P

where nₛ is synchronous speed in rpm, f is frequency in Hz and P is the number of poles.

Typical causes

  • Generation-load imbalance
  • Large system disturbance
  • Grid separation
  • Governor/turbine control problems
  • Measurement/PT issues

Frequency schemes commonly use pickup thresholds and time delays, and some systems use multiple stages for controlled load shedding or generator/system protection. Exact stages are plant/system specific.

10. 50 / 51 — Overcurrent Protection

ANSI 50 represents instantaneous overcurrent and ANSI 51 represents time-delayed/inverse-time overcurrent. The exact implementation depends on the relay and protection coordination philosophy.

50 — Instantaneous

Operates when current exceeds a defined instantaneous threshold, subject to the relay's logic and measurement conditions.

51 — Time Overcurrent

Uses a time-current characteristic so higher current generally results in faster operation within the selected characteristic.

Why generator overcurrent is not usually considered alone

A generator can supply very high fault current during an external fault, so coordination with generator differential, feeder protection, transformer protection and system protection is essential. CT saturation and fault location also affect the measured current.

Practical checks

  • Which phase operated?
  • What was the current magnitude?
  • Was the fault inside or outside the generator protection zone?
  • Did another protection operate first?
  • Was CT saturation or wiring trouble possible?

11. Trip Matrix & 86 Lockout

Protection detection is only one part of generator protection. The trip matrix defines what happens after a protection element operates. ANSI 86 is commonly associated with a lockout function that requires deliberate reset after a serious trip, depending on the plant design.

Typical protection chain

Fault / abnormal condition → Protection relay element → Trip logic → 86 lockout / direct trip as designed → Generator breaker trip → Prime mover / field / auxiliary actions as designed → Alarm & event recording → Investigation
StageQuestion for the engineer

Critical: Never assume that every generator has the same 86 logic. Use the actual plant trip matrix, cause-and-effect diagram, relay logic and approved operating procedure.

12. CT / PT Failure & Protection Misoperation

A protection relay can only make a correct decision if its measurement inputs are correct. CT and PT/VT problems can therefore create false operation, failure to operate, or misleading indications.

CT-related problems

  • Open CT secondary circuit
  • Wrong polarity
  • Incorrect ratio/tap configuration
  • Loose termination
  • Wiring/core selection problems
  • CT saturation during faults

PT/VT-related problems

  • Fuse/MCB failure where applicable
  • Loose wiring
  • Wrong phase connection
  • Incorrect ratio
  • Loss of one phase
  • Abnormal secondary voltage

How to distinguish a real fault from measurement trouble

Compare the relay record with independent plant measurements. For example, compare relay voltage/current with another healthy meter, check the affected phase, examine sudden step changes, inspect alarms for VT/CT supervision and review the event waveform. This does not replace physical inspection or the approved test procedure.

Golden rule: Never disconnect or open a live CT secondary circuit. Follow the plant's approved isolation and test procedure and use appropriate test equipment.

13. Practical Generator Trip Analysis

When a generator trips, the most important task is to identify the first initiating protection or abnormal condition, not simply the last alarm seen on the operator screen.

Recommended investigation sequence

StepWhat to examine
1Exact trip time and sequence-of-events timestamps
2First protection element / relay target
3Generator MW, MVAR, voltage, current and frequency immediately before trip
4Field voltage/current and AVR/excitation status
5Breaker status and 86 lockout status
6Turbine/prime-mover condition and governor response
7Relay oscillography and event record
8CT/PT supervision and measurement integrity
9Plant process conditions and operator actions
10Root-cause investigation before reset/re-energization

Example: apparent 87G trip

Suppose the generator trips and 87G appears on the alarm screen. Do not immediately conclude that the stator has failed. Check whether the relay saw a genuine differential current, whether restraint was normal, whether CT circuits were healthy, and what the oscillography shows. A CT circuit problem, wiring/polarity issue or relay/measurement problem can produce a protection operation or misleading indication.

Example: 32 reverse power

If 32 operates, compare MW direction with turbine input. Then check governor position, steam/fuel input, turbine trip signals and breaker status. The goal is to determine whether the generator genuinely entered motoring or whether the measurement/protection chain produced the indication.

Engineer mindset:
Do not ask only “Which relay tripped?”
Ask: “What happened first, what did the relay actually measure, what logic acted next, and what physical condition caused the measurement?”

Protection investigation checklist

  • Relay target / element
  • SOE timestamps
  • Oscillography
  • MW / MVAR / PF
  • Generator voltage and current
  • Field voltage and field current
  • AVR/excitation status
  • CT/PT health
  • Breaker and 86 status
  • Turbine/governor status
  • Approved trip matrix / cause-and-effect
  • Physical inspection and test results

🧠 15. Generator Trip Decision Tree

Use this simplified decision tree when learning how to investigate a generator trip. It is an educational model, not a replacement for the actual plant cause-and-effect diagram.

GENERATOR TRIPPED ↓ Check SOE / relay event time ↓ What was the FIRST protection indication? ↓ ┌───────────────┬───────────────┬───────────────┐ 87G / 64G 32 40 46 Internal / Reverse Loss of Negative earth fault power excitation sequence ↓ Then verify the measured quantities ↓ Was the measurement credible? ↓ CT / PT / relay / wiring check ↓ Confirm trip logic + 86 + breaker status ↓ Investigate physical root cause ↓ Approved restoration procedure

Five questions every engineer should ask

  1. What happened first? — Use SOE timestamps rather than screen-alarm order alone.
  2. What did the relay actually measure? — Check current, voltage, frequency, MW, MVAR and relay element data.
  3. Was the measurement trustworthy? — Consider CT/PT problems, wiring and supervision alarms.
  4. What logic operated next? — Check trip matrix, interlocks, 86 and breaker feedback.
  5. What physical event caused the electrical signature? — Connect the relay record to the turbine, generator, transformer and grid condition.

📈 18. How to Read a Generator Relay Event Record

A relay event report often contains more information than the simple alarm text displayed on the DCS. Learning to read the record is an important protection-engineering skill.

1. Digital channels

Look for breaker status, trip outputs, 86, AVR status, field breaker and other plant-specific binary signals.

2. Analog channels

Look for phase currents, voltages, frequency, MW/MVAR and other quantities recorded by the relay.

3. Protection elements

Identify when 87G, 64G, 46, 40, 32 or another element picked up and when it actually operated.

4. Sequence of events

Compare the timestamps of pickup, trip output, breaker opening and lockout operation.

Example investigation: t₀ abnormal current appears ↓ t₁ protection element pickup ↓ t₂ relay trip output ↓ t₃ 86 / trip logic response ↓ t₄ generator breaker opens ↓ t₅ turbine / excitation response ↓ t₆ alarms and stable post-trip condition

The actual order depends on the plant protection design. The objective is to reconstruct the event from recorded evidence rather than from assumptions.

🎯 Final Learning Path

To become strong in generator protection, learn each function in this order:

  1. Understand the generator electrical zones.
  2. Understand CTs and PTs and their locations.
  3. Learn 87G differential protection deeply.
  4. Learn stator and rotor earth-fault protection.
  5. Learn abnormal-condition protections such as 46, 40, 32 and 24.
  6. Understand voltage and frequency protection.
  7. Study 86 lockout and the complete trip matrix.
  8. Finally, practice reading relay event reports and SOE records.
Safety / engineering note: This page is an educational guide. Actual relay settings, trip logic, CT/PT connections, testing methods and re-energization decisions must come from the specific generator manufacturer, relay manual, approved protection study, plant drawings and operating/test procedures.
